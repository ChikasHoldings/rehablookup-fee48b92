Is My Loved One an Alcoholic? 12 Warning Signs to Watch For

By RehabLookup Editorial Team February 7, 2026 9 min read min read
Learn to recognize the critical warning signs of alcohol use disorder in someone you care about, and discover how to approach the conversation with compassion.

Watching someone you love struggle with alcohol can be one of the most painful experiences a family member or friend can face. The line between social drinking and alcohol use disorder is not always clear, which makes identifying a problem especially challenging. Understanding the warning signs is the first crucial step toward helping your loved one find the support they need.

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) affects approximately 29.5 million Americans according to the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism. Yet many families remain uncertain whether their loved one's drinking has crossed the line from occasional overindulgence to a serious health condition requiring professional treatment.

Understanding Alcohol Use Disorder

Alcohol use disorder is a medical condition characterized by an impaired ability to stop or control alcohol use despite adverse social, occupational, or health consequences. Unlike outdated stereotypes, alcoholism does not look the same in everyone—a high-functioning professional can struggle with AUD just as severely as someone whose life has visibly unraveled.

12 Warning Signs Your Loved One May Be an Alcoholic

1. Drinking alone or in secret: They hide bottles, drink in the car, or sneak drinks when they think no one is watching.

2. Increased tolerance: They need significantly more alcohol to feel the same effects they used to get from smaller amounts.

3. Neglecting responsibilities: Work performance has declined, bills go unpaid, or family obligations are frequently forgotten.

4. Withdrawal symptoms: When they haven't had a drink, they experience shakiness, sweating, anxiety, or nausea.

5. Loss of control: They consistently drink more than they intended or cannot stop once they start.

6. Continuing despite consequences: Even after alcohol-related problems (DUIs, health issues, relationship damage), the drinking continues.

7. Blackouts: They frequently cannot remember what happened while drinking.

8. Drinking in dangerous situations: They drive after drinking or combine alcohol with medications.

9. Mood changes: Increased irritability, depression, or anxiety that improves after drinking.

10. Physical changes: Unexplained weight changes, poor hygiene, bloodshot eyes, or broken capillaries on the face.

11. Time spent on alcohol: Much of their day revolves around obtaining, using, or recovering from alcohol.

12. Failed attempts to quit: They have expressed desire to cut back or stop but cannot maintain it.

How to Approach the Conversation

Confronting a loved one about their drinking requires careful preparation. Choose a time when they are sober, use "I" statements to express your concerns without blame, and be prepared for denial or defensiveness. Having information about treatment options ready can help if they show openness to getting help.

Professional intervention specialists can guide families through this process. Many treatment centers offer family support services to help navigate these difficult conversations.

Treatment Options Available

If your loved one is ready to seek help, multiple evidence-based treatment options exist. Medical detoxification may be necessary for those with severe alcohol dependence, as alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous without proper supervision. Following detox, residential inpatient treatment or intensive outpatient programs can provide the structured support needed for lasting recovery.

Finding the right treatment center is essential. Look for facilities with accredited programs, experienced staff, and aftercare planning to support long-term sobriety.

Taking the Next Step

Recognizing that someone you love may have a drinking problem is difficult, but it is also the first step toward helping them. Remember that you cannot force someone to get sober, but you can offer support, set healthy boundaries, and be ready with resources when they are ready to accept help.
