Can You Force Someone Into Rehab? Understanding Involuntary Treatment Laws

By RehabLookup Editorial Team April 4, 2026 11 min read min read
Explore the legal options for involuntary addiction treatment including the Marchman Act, Caseys Law, and state commitment laws.

The question of whether you can legally force someone into addiction treatment is complex and varies significantly by state. While voluntary treatment generally produces better outcomes, involuntary treatment can be lifesaving in certain situations.

Involuntary commitment laws by state

Approximately 37 states plus the District of Columbia have some form of involuntary commitment law for substance use disorders. These laws allow family members, healthcare providers, or courts to petition for mandatory treatment when a person poses a danger to themselves or others due to addiction.

Major involuntary commitment laws

  • Marchman Act (Florida): allows family members, healthcare providers, or three unrelated adults to petition the court for involuntary assessment and treatment
  • Caseys Law (Kentucky): allows parents, relatives, or friends to petition the court to order involuntary treatment
  • Ricks Law (Indiana): allows family members to petition for involuntary commitment for substance use
  • Section 35 (Massachusetts): allows doctors, police, judges, and family members to petition for involuntary commitment

How the involuntary commitment process works

  • File a petition with the court describing the persons condition
  • A judge reviews the petition and may order an assessment
  • A clinical assessment determines if involuntary treatment is appropriate
  • The court issues an order for treatment (typically 60 to 90 days)
  • The person is transported to a treatment facility
  • Treatment includes detox, therapy, and stabilization

Does forced rehab work?

Research shows that involuntary treatment can be effective. Studies indicate that outcomes for involuntary patients are comparable to voluntary patients when treatment is of sufficient length and quality. Many people who initially resist treatment later express gratitude for the intervention.

Involuntary treatment should be considered as a last resort when all voluntary options have been exhausted and the persons life is in danger.

Alternatives to forced treatment

  • Professional intervention (90+ percent success rate in getting someone to accept treatment)
  • Drug court programs that offer treatment as an alternative to incarceration
  • Community-based outreach and engagement
  • Motivational interviewing to build willingness
  • Family therapy and systemic approaches

Ethical considerations

Involuntary treatment raises important ethical questions about autonomy and personal rights. The legal standard requires demonstrating that the person is a danger to themselves or others due to substance use. Treatment must be the least restrictive option and must include due process protections.

Frequently asked questions

Can parents force an adult child into rehab?

Parents can petition for involuntary treatment in states that have commitment laws. The process requires court approval and clinical assessment. Simply being an adult child does not override due process requirements.

How long can someone be held in involuntary treatment?

Court orders typically range from 60 to 90 days. Extensions may be possible with additional court approval. The treatment facility can recommend discharge earlier if clinically appropriate.
