Can You Force Someone Into Rehab? Legal Options and Better Alternatives

By RehabLookup Editorial Team February 2026 11 min read min read
When someone refuses help, families feel desperate. Here's what the law actually allows, which states have involuntary commitment, and approaches that work better.

Watching someone you love destroy themselves while refusing help is one of the most painful experiences a family can face. When reasoning fails, when pleading doesn't work, when every conversation ends in anger—the question becomes unavoidable: Can we make them go?

The answer is complicated. In some states, yes, you can legally force an adult into treatment. But whether you should—and whether it works—requires a deeper look.

The Legal Landscape: Involuntary Commitment Laws

Most states have some form of involuntary commitment law, but they vary dramatically. Generally, these laws require proving that someone is an immediate danger to themselves or others due to substance use—and that bar is intentionally high.

States with Specific Addiction Commitment Laws

About 37 states have laws allowing involuntary commitment specifically for substance use disorders, though requirements and processes differ. Florida's Marchman Act is the most well-known, allowing family members to petition a court for emergency or long-term involuntary treatment.

Other states with notable addiction commitment statutes include California, Texas, Kentucky, Ohio, and Massachusetts. Each has different criteria, timeframes, and procedures.

How the Process Typically Works

  • Family member or concerned party files a petition with the court
  • Court may order evaluation or emergency detention
  • Medical professional assesses whether criteria are met
  • If criteria are satisfied, court orders treatment for a specified period
  • Treatment typically lasts 60-90 days, with possible extensions

The process is not simple. It involves attorneys, court appearances, and significant documentation. And the person being committed has legal rights, including the right to contest the commitment.

Does Forced Treatment Work?

This is where honest conversation gets difficult. Research on involuntary treatment shows mixed results.

Some studies show that coerced treatment can lead to positive outcomes—particularly when the alternative is death or incarceration. Getting someone medically stabilized and into an environment where they might eventually engage with treatment is better than doing nothing while they overdose.

But other research suggests that intrinsic motivation is crucial for lasting recovery. Someone who doesn't want to be in treatment may go through the motions without making real changes. And the resentment from being forced can damage family relationships further.

The Case for Voluntary Alternatives

Before pursuing legal compulsion, most addiction professionals recommend exhausting voluntary approaches—not because they always work, but because engagement matters enormously for recovery outcomes.

Professional Intervention

A structured intervention with a professional interventionist gives you the best chance of getting voluntary agreement. Modern intervention methods have moved beyond confrontation toward compassionate conversation. Success rates for professionally facilitated interventions range from 80-90% in getting someone to agree to treatment.

CRAFT Method

Community Reinforcement and Family Training (CRAFT) teaches family members communication strategies that encourage treatment-seeking behavior without ultimatums. Research shows CRAFT helps nearly 70% of resistant individuals enter treatment voluntarily within six months.

Waiting for a Crisis

Sometimes, the most powerful motivator is hitting a personal bottom—an arrest, a health scare, a job loss, a relationship ending. Having treatment options ready when that moment comes can mean the difference between seizing the opportunity and missing it.

When Involuntary Commitment Makes Sense

There are situations where pursuing involuntary treatment is appropriate:

  • Immediate risk of death from overdose or severe withdrawal
  • Active psychosis or inability to make rational decisions
  • History of near-fatal overdoses with continued use
  • Failed multiple voluntary treatment attempts
  • Danger to others (especially children in the home)

In these circumstances, forced treatment—even imperfect treatment—can save a life and create an opportunity for eventual voluntary engagement.

Practical Considerations

If you're considering involuntary commitment, know what you're getting into:

  • The process is stressful and can take weeks
  • Your family member may not forgive you immediately
  • Treatment programs willing to accept involuntary patients are limited
  • Insurance may not cover court-ordered treatment
  • You'll need to be prepared to follow through on aftercare planning

Finding Your Way Forward

There's no perfect answer for families in this situation. Forced treatment is a tool—sometimes necessary, always imperfect. Whatever path you choose, remember that your own wellbeing matters too. Family support groups, therapy, and Al-Anon can help you cope regardless of what your loved one decides.

And know that recovery happens every day, often after years of struggle. The situation that seems hopeless today can look different tomorrow.
